Brackenford — The Seven Bells
An open-world archery game. Desktop and browser VR, one file.

PLAY ON THIS COMPUTER
Open Brackenford-Seven-Bells.html in Chrome or Edge and choose Enter the vale.
Mouse looks, W A S D walks, Shift runs, Space jumps. Hold the left mouse button to draw, release to loose. Arrows drop over distance, so aim above far targets.
M opens the map. Click a bell you have already woken to travel to it. Esc pauses.
Progress saves in the browser. Start over on the title card clears it.

THE RANGE (BROWSER BUILD)
The game now opens on the range: you stand at the shooting line and do not walk. The title card switches between The range and Roam the vale.
Twenty stages, each with a fixed number of arrows: longer distances, steady wind, gusts, sliding marks and pop-ups that lie down. An orange pennant by the line points downwind and stretches as the wind rises; the board shows its strength.
Scoring: inner rings and longer shots are worth more. Hits in a row build a streak that raises the score and plays a rising scale; a miss ends it. Letting go just as the draw completes is a perfect release and adds a quarter (on desktop the draw bar turns white; in VR the controllers pulse at full draw).
A stage ends when the arrows are spent. One to three stars; one star opens the next stage. Then shoot the BACK, AGAIN or NEXT sign, or press B, R or N. Best scores and stars are saved.
Replay: a stage ends with one slowed replay. On the shorter stages it is the best bullseye of the stage, or failing that the final arrow: wherever it hit, or held beside the mark if it missed by 4 m or less. A final arrow that lands further away than that has no replay. The view starts on the archer at the line, follows the arrow down the lane and ends on the hit with its score. On desktop the camera cuts there; in VR you are set down beside the mark behind a short fade and put back at the line afterwards. A click, Space, Enter or a trigger pull skips it.
On a long lane (any stage with a mark at 40 m or more) there is always exactly one replay: the arrow that came closest to the middle of a mark, hit or miss. A miss is followed in, held still as it passes the mark, and labelled with how wide it went.
Power: how far you draw sets how fast the arrow leaves, from about 28 m/s at the lightest to 62 m/s at full draw. In VR, pull the string hand less far for a softer shot; the plaque on the bow shows the draw in tenths. On desktop, let go early, or roll the mouse wheel while drawing to ease the string forward or back and hold it there; the bar under the hint shows quarter marks and the draw as a percentage. A draw held with the wheel does not count as a perfect release.
Bow and handedness: the bow is a plain straight bow. The nocked arrow lies against the side of the grip, resting just above the bow hand, as on a real bow; before, it ran through the middle of the grip. Right-handed (bow in the left hand, arrow on the left of the bow) is the default; the title card and the H key switch to left-handed, which moves the bow to the right of the view and the arrow to the right of the bow. In VR the hand that takes the bow decides the side. A test confirmed the arrow shaft touches no part of the bow at rest, half draw and full draw, both ways round and in either hand in simulated VR; two still pictures were looked at. The Unity project still has the arrow through the grip and no handedness setting.
The grip is handed: a leather wrap with a swell that fills the palm on one side, and a small arrow rest on the other, under the shaft. It is mirrored for left-handed play and, in VR, for whichever hand holds the bow.
VR draw modes: the bow is put in your hand when VR starts (the left for a right-handed archer, the right for a left-handed one); a grip squeeze near the bow still swaps hands. Two-handed draw is the default: bring the free hand to the string, hold its trigger, pull back and let go; how far you pull sets the power, and a trigger pulled anywhere else does nothing but show a reminder. Single-handed draw: hold any trigger and the bow draws by itself in under a second; let go and the arrow flies straight out of the bow, and a short tap is a soft shot. Choose on the title card, or click the right stick in the headset. While drawing two-handed, the string hand is lightly smoothed and the bow turns to lie along the arrow, keeping only its lean from the wrist, so twisting the bow hand or jitter near the face does not turn the shot. Other buttons: grip swaps the bow hand and holds climbing pegs, the left stick walks when roaming and its click runs, the right stick turns. All of this was tested with simulated controllers only.
Coins: every range stage pays coins when it ends: one for every 20 points scored, plus 25 for each star earned on that stage for the first time. The total is on the board, the status panel and the bow plaque, and is saved. A save from before coins existed is paid once for the stars and best scores it already holds. There is nothing to spend them on yet; the shop comes later in Chapter 2. A test covered a first clear, a repeat clear paying no second star bonus, a weak run, saving and reloading, and the one-off payment to an old save.
Chapter 2, The Blight (browser build, stages 21 to 25 so far): shades have crept into the vale, hare-shaped things of dark thorn with ember eyes, horns and a spined back, wrapped in violet smoke. An arrow banishes one in a burst of embers. Sitting shades (stages 21, 22) slink off if an arrow lands within 3 m and come back somewhere else a few seconds later. Hiding shades (23) sit in tall grass with only their horns showing, rustle now and then, and are gone for good if startled. Creeping shades (24) cross open ground between grass clumps and sink into them. Running shades (25) dash and stop, and must be led. A stage ends when the arrows are spent or every shade is banished; the best one is replayed. All shapes are made in code. A scripted test covered each behaviour, the early end, the replay, scoring and stars, a body shot missing a hidden shade, and a led shot at a running one; still pictures were looked at. Nobody has played these stages and their difficulty is a guess. The shades do not threaten the player yet. Still to come: flying shades, a boss, the shop. (An earlier plan had gentle spirits that were woken and freed; the evil shades replaced it at the owner's choice, and the asset list in the assets folder still describes the gentle version.)
Asset tool: tools/make-assets.mjs turns the list in assets/asset-list.json into 3D models, by way of a concept picture from OpenAI's image model and then Tripo's image-to-3D. It needs your own TRIPO_API_KEY and OPENAI_API_KEY in the environment and has never been run against either service. The game cannot load GLB files yet.
Modelled art: the shade hare is now a real model. ChatGPT drew it from the approved concept image (assets/concepts), Tripo's image-to-3D turned the picture into a model of 1.95 million faces (kept in assets/source, 63 MB, not shipped), and two Blender scripts in tools cut it to 5,000 flat-coloured faces and wrote it as assets/shade-hare.json (140 KB), which the build step puts inside the game. tools/reduce-model.py does the reducing and colouring; tools/export-model.py splits head from body at the neck and writes the file. The head is a separate piece so the game can turn it: a shade looks at the archer every few seconds and just before it sets off, and its eyes flare as it does. No skeleton or rig is used. Tripo export needs a paid plan, which the owner bought. Looked at in still pictures only: the head turn and the eye flare have not been watched in motion, and at this size in a headset the model is unverified.
Rigged hare: in Tripo the model was retopologised to 4,412 faces (35 credits) and auto-rigged with the Other skeleton type (20 credits), giving 25 bones and no animations. tools/export-rigged.py writes it as assets/shade-hare-rig.json with bone weights, and the game builds a skinned mesh from it. All movement is written in code (shadePose in src/game.js): a bounding gait driven by the haunches with the chest counter-pitching, hind legs kicking and folding, forelegs reaching, the neck and head turning to look at the archer, ears that sway, trail, prick and flatten joint by joint with random twitches, a rear-up when banished. Tripo put both forelegs on one bone chain, which the code works around. Moving shades now choose their own next stop and pace each time: runners roam an area with random pauses and feints, creepers pick among the grass clumps. The smoke bubbles are gone; the glowing eyes mark a shade. Five frozen poses were looked at and the skin bends without tearing; the motion itself has not been watched, the model is sculpted sitting so a running shade is a sitting hare bounding, and at 20 m and beyond a shade is small and dark.
Choosing a stage: the title card has a Stage list showing every stage with its stars and best score; picking one starts it (and returns to the range if you were roaming). On desktop, [ and ] (or Page Up and Page Down) step to the previous and next stage at any time except while drawing. In VR, flick the left stick left or right on the range. Every stage is open to choose while the game is in development; stars still unlock the NEXT sign as before. A test covered the list, the keys, both ends, a fresh quiver on changing, the block while drawing, returning from roaming, stars appearing in the list, and the VR stick stepping once per flick.
Winged shades (stages 26 to 29): a stand-in built in code until a modelled one is made, with a dark body, ember eyes and two flat wings that beat. Perching shades (26) sit on posts and turn to look at you; an arrow passing within about 2 m lifts one off, and it comes back after a few seconds. Lurking shades (27) hide in bushes showing only a glint of their eyes and a stir of leaves; an arrow landing within 3.5 m flushes one into a climbing flight where it can be hit, and left alone each loses patience and goes of its own accord. Crossing shades (28) fly back and forth over the lane, a new height, side and speed each pass. Stage 29 mixes hares and winged shades. A test covered each behaviour, a led shot in the air, the mixed stage and the replay; two views were looked at. Not played by a person; the flying ones are small and fast and may be too hard.
Release mode: src/config.json sets whether the build is the release, its title, and which stages it plays in what order. In release mode the game shows that title, plays the 17 chosen stages, offers only stages already unlocked, and hides roaming and coins; all 29 stages stay in the code. Adding ?release=0 to the address shows everything as before, and ?fps=1 shows frames a second on screen, on the board and on the bow plaque. The first stage is guided, one instruction at a time, on the page and on a low board in VR, and a learner who has not yet hit anything is not left without arrows. If the game breaks, a banner asks the player to reload. Stages.md lists every stage (written by tools/stage-table.mjs). Release-checklist.md, Day-1-test-sheet.md and Release-page-text.md cover the release.
Tests: open tests/all.html over http to run every suite; it prints one line each. They need the folder served, for example with python -m http.server.

TRAINING YARD AND CLIMBING (BROWSER BUILD)
A new game starts facing the training yard, just north-west of the hamlet. A star on the compass, a square on the map and (in VR) the gold needle lead to it until training is done.
A board beside the shooting line shows the current lesson. There are five: hit the near round three times; hit the rounds at 20, 40 and 70 m; hit the sliding round three times; climb the low wall and then the tower; hit the far round from the top of the tower.
Climbing on desktop: walk into a pegged face and hold W. S climbs down, A and D shuffle sideways, Space lets go. The bow is put away while you climb.
Climbing in VR: hold a grip (or close a fist, with hand tracking) on a pegged face and pull yourself up hand over hand. Get your head above the top and let go to step onto it. Letting go lower down drops you.
Training progress saves with the rest of the game. Falling does no harm yet.

THE VALE
About 750 m across: a hamlet in the middle, a lake with an isle, a tor, a deepwood, an old keep, a ridge and a south meadow.
Seven bells, each marked by a column of light until you wake it. Diamonds on the compass point to the ones still asleep.
24 straw rounds (three on the hamlet range) and 10 drifting lanterns are scattered around and are not on the map.
Deer graze and bolt when you come close or an arrow lands near them. The day runs from dawn to twilight in 14 minutes.

THE BOW AND THE ARROW
An arrow sits nocked on the string and slides back with the draw; the limbs bend, and on desktop the view tightens a little. Holding a full draw makes the bow tremble slightly; it does not change where the arrow goes.
On release the string rings, the bow kicks forward and a fresh arrow comes up a moment later. The arrow fishtails off the string, steadies, spins and leaves a short pale streak.
Arrows bury their heads and quiver where they stick, and throw up chips of earth, straw, bark or water. An arrow glances off a bell and tumbles to the ground; it flies on through a lantern.
The reticle flashes on a hit. In VR the string hand ticks as you draw and the bow hand thumps on release, on controllers that support haptics.
Arrow speed, gravity and hit tests are unchanged from the first version.

VR CONTROLS
Left stick walks in the direction you face; click it to run. Right stick turns in 30 degree steps.
Squeeze the grip near the bow to take it with either hand; squeeze with the other hand to swap.
Bring the other controller to the string, hold its trigger, pull back and release.
The plaque on the bow shows your counts, and the gold needle above it points to the nearest sleeping bell.

HAND TRACKING
Put the controllers down on a headset with hand tracking and the game follows your hands, drawn as dots at each joint.
Close a fist on the bow to take it. To pass it across, open the bow hand and close the other hand on the bow.
Pinch the string, or close a fist on it, pull back and open the hand to loose.
Raise your free hand and point a finger: you walk the way it points. There is no snap turn with hands, so turn your body. Hands give no vibration.
In the Unity project the same gestures work on a PC VR runtime that reports hand tracking, while no controller is tracked. It uses the XR Hands package (1.7.3); Brackenford > Enable hand tracking switches on the OpenXR Hand Tracking Subsystem feature, and it also runs by itself when the project opens.

The browser VR path needs the HTML served from an HTTPS origin (or localhost) in a WebXR headset browser.

UNITY PROJECT
In Unity Hub, add the BrackenfordSevenBells folder. It targets Unity 6000.6.7f1 with the Windows OpenXR loader, Input System and Oculus Touch interaction profile, the same setup as the Bell Trial project. Allow the first package import to finish, open Assets/Scenes/SevenBells.unity and press Play.
The whole vale is generated on entering Play, from the same noise and random sequence as the browser build, so both builds have the same terrain, bells, straw rounds and lanterns. Controls match the browser build on desktop and in VR. Without an active headset the scene uses the desktop controls; for PC VR, connect a headset through its Windows OpenXR runtime.
Progress saves in PlayerPrefs. The Brackenford menu has Check the vale, which logs what the generator placed.

CHECKED IN THE UNITY PROJECT
The editor opened the project in batch mode: scripts and both shaders compiled, and the generated bell heights, straw round and lantern positions matched the browser build.
A Play-mode smoke test (Assets/Editor/PlayCheck.cs) entered Play, loosed one arrow at the hamlet bell and woke it, with no errors from the game.
The bow and arrow rework is ported to the Unity scripts; they compile and the smoke test passed again afterwards. That test only proves a shot still wakes the bell: none of the new animation, chips, streak or haptics has been seen or felt in Unity.
Hand tracking in Unity: the XR Hands package imported, the scripts compile with it, the Hand Tracking Subsystem feature is switched on in the OpenXR settings, and the smoke test passed again. The gesture code is a port of the browser version and has not been run with any hand data, real or made up.
Both runs were headless, so nothing was looked at on screen: the look of the terrain, sky, text and HUD, the mouse and keyboard feel, and everything in VR still need a playtest in the editor and on a headset. No player build was made.
On first import from a very deep folder path, Unity may report failed imports for some Input System editor files; a shorter path avoids that.
